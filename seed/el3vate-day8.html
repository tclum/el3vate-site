Prototyping solutions

Fifteen disciplines, fifteen assignments. Each one has something students make with their hands, something they build with AI, and a four-week plan that fits inside a real semester.

How to use this page

Find your field. Read the four-week plan. Copy the prompt into Claude or ChatGPT and change the bracketed parts to fit the course you are actually teaching.

Nothing here needs you to learn a tool first. The physical builds go through PACE as a service: your students submit a file, we fabricate it, nobody needs equipment training. The AI builds happen in a chat window.

Every plan assumes you will use one class session on this, not a semester. If a four-week module is too much, run week two on its own and you still have a real assignment.

What PACE provides

  • 3D printingFree for UH faculty. Students submit an .stl file; we print and return it.

Cut sheet · 15 parts · one per discipline

Part 01 / 15

Political Science

For the challenge named in this cohort: getting entrepreneurship into political theory without it feeling bolted on.

Make it Laser cut · engrave

Students cut a real Hawaiʻi district or precinct as movable tiles, then physically redraw the boundaries to satisfy competing constraints (population equality, community of interest, incumbent protection). The map stops being an abstraction the moment they have to move a piece and tell the room who they just moved.

Build it AI chat · no code

A stakeholder simulator for a bill actually moving through the Hawaiʻi legislature. The AI plays a lobbyist, an agency head, and a constituent group in turn; students have to get a provision through and then account for what they traded away.

Four weeks, realistically

  1. Wk 1Pick one live bill from the current session. Students write a one-page stake map: who wins, who loses, who is invisible in the text.
  2. Wk 2Build and run the stakeholder simulator in class. Students log the three moments the AI’s stakeholder said something they could not verify against the actual record. That log is the graded artifact, not the transcript.
  3. Wk 3Submit the cut file for the district tiles (due Monday, so the 7–10 day turnaround lands before finals). While it fabricates, students draft the defense of their map.
  4. Wk 4Physical redraw session and public defense. Each team hands their tiles to another team, who must find the choice that cannot be justified.

Starter prompt

You are running a legislative negotiation simulation for an undergraduate political science class. I will give you a bill. Play three stakeholders in turn: a paid lobbyist for the affected industry, a state agency director who has to implement it, and a community group that was not consulted. Stay in character, push back hard, and do not concede quickly. After each exchange, stop and ask me what I am willing to give up. Do not summarize or break character unless I type PAUSE. The bill is: [paste bill text or summary here].

Part 02 / 15

Entrepreneurship

The business model canvas from Day 4 stays theoretical until a stranger holds the thing and says no.

Make it 3D print · laser cut

A physical MVP good enough to put in a customer’s hands. Not a finished product: a form study, a fit test, a packaging mockup. The point is that customer discovery interviews change completely when there is an object on the table.

Build it AI chat · no code

A working landing page for the venture, built in one prompt, with an email capture and three headline variants. Students run traffic from their own networks and report the actual conversion rate, not their opinion of it.

Four weeks, realistically

  1. Wk 1Teams commit to one value proposition and write the riskiest assumption underneath it in a single sentence.
  2. Wk 2Build the landing page live in class. Submit the print or cut file the same week so fabrication runs in parallel with the digital test.
  3. Wk 3Run the test: fifteen real interviews and whatever traffic they can generate. No pivoting yet, just collection.
  4. Wk 4Prototype in hand, data in hand, teams present what they were wrong about. Grade the quality of the disconfirmation, not the size of the number.

Starter prompt

Build me a single-page landing site for a new product, as one self-contained HTML file I can host anywhere. The product is: [describe it in two sentences]. The customer is: [describe them]. Include a headline, three short benefit statements, an email signup field, and nothing else. Then give me two alternate headlines that make a meaningfully different promise, not just reworded versions of the first. Keep the copy plain and specific; no marketing filler.

Part 03 / 15

Law

Assessment that an AI cannot sit for: the student has to speak, be interrupted, and recover.

Make it Hana recording studio

Students record oral argument or client counseling in the studio, then annotate their own recording. Production quality matters here: a clean recording makes students confront delivery, pacing and filler in a way a classroom run-through never does.

Build it AI chat · no code

An opposing counsel that never gets tired. Students argue a motion against an AI instructed to press the weakest point in their position and to interrupt. The unglamorous benefit is volume: every student gets ten rounds instead of one.

Four weeks, realistically

  1. Wk 1Assign one fact pattern. Students brief it the traditional way. No AI yet.
  2. Wk 2AI opposing counsel rounds, done as homework, minimum five exchanges. Students submit the transcript with their own margin notes marking where they conceded too fast.
  3. Wk 3Studio recordings, scheduled in pairs. Ten minutes of argument, five of counseling.
  4. Wk 4Students watch their own recording against the AI transcript and write a one-page delivery diagnosis. That reflection is the assessment.

Starter prompt

Act as opposing counsel in a moot court exercise for a law student. Here is the fact pattern and my position: [paste]. Your job is to find the weakest link in my argument and attack it repeatedly. Interrupt me when I am being evasive. Do not be collegial, do not concede a point unless I actually earn it, and do not offer to help me improve my argument. Ask one question at a time and wait for my answer.

Part 04 / 15

Comparative Philosophy

The AI is the object of study, not the tool. Students are graded on catching where it flattens a tradition.

Make it Laser cut · engrave

A physical apparatus that forces a commitment: engraved tiles for competing ethical frameworks that students must physically arrange and rank against a case, or a switchboard where the abstract choice becomes a lever you actually have to pull in front of people.

Build it AI chat · no code

An interlocutor instructed to argue from one specific tradition. The assignment is adversarial: students interrogate it against primary sources and document every place it substitutes a generic Western liberal position for the tradition it claims to hold. A student who has not read the text cannot do this.

Four weeks, realistically

  1. Wk 1Choose two traditions and one shared case. Students read the primary sources first. The AI stays closed.
  2. Wk 2Students build their interlocutor prompt and run it. They keep a failure log: quote from the AI, quote from the source, one sentence on what got lost.
  3. Wk 3Design the physical artifact. What distinction between the two traditions can only be shown by arranging objects in space? Cut file due end of week.
  4. Wk 4Present the artifact and the failure log together. The claim being assessed: here is what the machine could not hold, and here is why it matters.

Starter prompt

You are going to argue a case from within a single philosophical tradition, and I am going to check your work. Adopt the position of [name the tradition, e.g. classical Confucian ethics] as it is actually articulated in its primary texts, not a modern popularization. The case is: [describe it]. Argue the position fully. Do not hedge, do not present multiple perspectives, and do not add a balanced summary at the end. When I quote a primary source back at you that contradicts what you said, address the contradiction directly rather than conceding everything.

Part 05 / 15

Marketing / Strategic Communication

Students generate ten campaigns in an hour, then have to defend why nine of them are bad.

Make it 3D print · laser cut

Physical packaging or a point-of-sale piece for a real local client. Holding a mockup surfaces every decision a slide deck hides: size, weight, what the customer reads first from arm’s length.

Build it AI chat · no code

A message-testing harness. Students generate a wide spread of positioning variants, then build a simple landing page per variant and measure. The AI supplies volume; the student supplies the judgment about which variant is worth defending.

Four weeks, realistically

  1. Wk 1Land one real client, even a campus organization. Written brief, signed by the client.
  2. Wk 2Generate wide with AI in class, then cut down to three positions with a written rationale per cut. Submit packaging cut file.
  3. Wk 3Build one page per position, run whatever traffic is available for five days.
  4. Wk 4Client presentation with the physical mockup and the actual numbers. The client picks. Students debrief the gap between their favorite and the client’s.

Starter prompt

Generate twelve distinct positioning statements for this product, aimed at this audience: [describe product and audience]. Make them genuinely different from each other in strategy, not in wording: vary what need is being claimed, what the competition is, and what the customer has to believe. Number them. Then for each one, write the single sentence a skeptical customer would say to reject it.

Part 06 / 15

Nutrition & Dietetics

Counseling practice at volume, plus a teaching tool students can hand to an actual community group.

Make it 3D print

Portion and plate-method models that students design for a specific community they are working with, in the foods that community actually eats. Generic food models exist commercially; models built around local staples do not, and making that argument is half the assignment.

Build it AI chat · no code

A standardized client for motivational interviewing practice: someone with a real cultural food context, a budget constraint, and a reason to resist. Students run the session repeatedly, which is impossible with human volunteers.

Four weeks, realistically

  1. Wk 1Students select a community partner and a specific counseling scenario. Draft the client profile they will need.
  2. Wk 2Build the AI client and run three sessions, submitting transcripts. Grade whether the student did more listening than telling.
  3. Wk 3Design and submit the physical teaching model. Requirement: justify every food choice against the partner community.
  4. Wk 4Field test the model with the partner and revise once based on what confused people.

Starter prompt

Role-play as a nutrition counseling client for a dietetics student. You are [age, situation, cultural food background, budget constraint]. You are here because someone else told you to come, and you are polite but not really convinced you need to change anything. Do not volunteer information unless I ask a good question. Do not become enthusiastic and cooperative just because I say something encouraging. Stay in character until I type END SESSION, then give me feedback on whether I actually listened.

Part 07 / 15

Urban Planning

A physical model changes a community meeting more than any rendering does.

Make it Laser cut · layered

A layered site model of a real Honolulu parcel: topography, existing structures, proposed massing, each on its own removable layer. Residents can lift the proposal off and see what is underneath, which is exactly the conversation that renderings prevent.

Build it AI chat · no code

An interactive tradeoff explainer for the same parcel: move a slider for height or density and see the consequences a resident actually cares about, in plain language. Built in an afternoon, used in a real meeting.

Four weeks, realistically

  1. Wk 1Pick one contested parcel with public documentation. Students extract the actual constraints from the record.
  2. Wk 2Build the interactive explainer. Test it on someone outside the major and rebuild the parts they did not understand.
  3. Wk 3Cut file due. While it fabricates, students prepare the meeting facilitation, not the presentation.
  4. Wk 4Run a mock community meeting with the model and the tool on the table. Invite the neighborhood board if the timing allows.

Starter prompt

Build me a single self-contained HTML page with sliders that shows planning tradeoffs for a site. The variables are: [building height, unit count, parking ratio, open space, whatever applies]. When a slider moves, update plain-language consequences that a resident would care about: shadow, traffic, who can afford to live there, what gets demolished. No jargon, no acronyms. Assume the reader has never been to a planning meeting.

Part 08 / 15

Learning Design & Technology

Build the learning object, then audit it for accessibility before anyone sees it.

Make it 3D print · laser cut

A tactile manipulative for a concept students currently teach with a diagram. The design constraint that makes this a real assignment: it has to work for a learner who cannot see the diagram.

Build it AI chat · no code

A working interactive module built in one sitting, then run against an accessibility checklist. The audit is where the learning is. Most AI-generated interfaces fail keyboard navigation and contrast on the first pass, and finding that is the skill.

Four weeks, realistically

  1. Wk 1Choose one concept and write the learning objective before touching a tool. Objective gets peer-reviewed for measurability.
  2. Wk 2Build the digital module with AI. Ship a rough version fast.
  3. Wk 3Accessibility audit against WCAG basics: keyboard only, contrast, screen reader, reduced motion. Document every failure and fix it. Submit fabrication file for the tactile version.
  4. Wk 4User test both versions with three learners outside the class. Revise once. The revision log is the deliverable.

Starter prompt

Build a single self-contained HTML page that teaches this concept interactively: [concept]. The learner is [describe them]. Requirements: it must be fully operable by keyboard alone, meet WCAG AA contrast, respect prefers-reduced-motion, and work on a phone. After you build it, list every accessibility decision you made and every place you are uncertain whether it passes.

Part 09 / 15

Bioinformatics

The structure in your hand answers a question the rotating model on screen does not.

Make it 3D print

A printed structure from a record the student selected and defended: a binding site, a mutation, a fold. Print two variants, wild type and mutant, and let the difference be something you can feel rather than something you are told.

Build it AI chat · no code

An interactive explainer of the student’s own analysis, written for a non-specialist. Explaining a pipeline to a general audience exposes whether the student understands it, which a working script does not.

Four weeks, realistically

  1. Wk 1Each student picks a structure or dataset and writes the biological question in one sentence a non-scientist would understand.
  2. Wk 2Run the analysis. Prepare and submit the print file, including the mutant variant.
  3. Wk 3Build the public-facing interactive explainer. Peer review is done by a student from a different discipline who has to say what they did not follow.
  4. Wk 4Present both. Assessment weight sits on whether the non-specialist reviewer understood the finding.

Starter prompt

Build a single self-contained HTML page that explains this analysis to someone with no biology background: [describe the analysis and the finding]. Use an interactive element so the reader can change one input and see the result change. Explain every term the first time it appears. Do not use an acronym without spelling it out. Then tell me the three places where you had to simplify in a way that a specialist would object to.

Part 10 / 15

English Literature

The strongest case for humanities in an AI world is a student demonstrating exactly where the machine reads badly.

Make it Hana recording studio

A produced literary podcast episode or recorded close reading. Audio rewards the things the discipline actually teaches: pacing, emphasis, the pause before the word that matters. It is also assessment that resists generated submission.

Build it AI chat · no code

A machine reading of a passage, generated deliberately, then dismantled in writing. Students document where the AI produced a plausible reading that the text does not support. This is a career argument as much as a literary one.

Four weeks, realistically

  1. Wk 1Close reading of an assigned passage, written by hand, submitted before any AI use.
  2. Wk 2Generate the AI reading of the same passage. Students annotate it against the text: what it noticed, what it invented, what it could not see.
  3. Wk 3Record the podcast episode in the studio, arguing the reading the AI missed.
  4. Wk 4Publish the episode with the annotated comparison as show notes. The artifact is portfolio-ready, which is the point.

Starter prompt

Give me a close reading of this passage: [paste passage]. Be specific and confident. Make claims about what the language is doing and why it matters. Do not hedge and do not tell me interpretations vary. Then, separately, list the three claims in your reading that you are least able to support with direct textual evidence.

Part 11 / 15

Finance

Students build the model in ten minutes, so the class time goes to arguing about the assumptions.

Make it Laser cut · engrave

Physical decision cards or a data object for a simulation: a term structure or a risk distribution rendered as something you can hold and compare side by side. Useful precisely because it slows the reading down.

Build it AI chat · no code

A working interactive model for a real decision, built live. The assignment is not the model. It is the sensitivity analysis and the written defense of every assumption the model needed to make.

Four weeks, realistically

  1. Wk 1Pick a real decision with public filings. Students list every assumption required, before building anything.
  2. Wk 2Build the interactive model with AI in class. Break it deliberately: what input makes the conclusion flip?
  3. Wk 3Sensitivity write-up plus fabrication of the physical comparison cards.
  4. Wk 4Defense session. Another team’s job is to find the assumption that carries the whole result.

Starter prompt

Build a single self-contained HTML page with an interactive model for this decision: [describe the decision]. Include sliders for every input that matters. Show the output updating live, and display the break-even point prominently. Then list every assumption you had to make to build it, and tell me which single assumption the conclusion is most sensitive to.

Part 12 / 15

Marriage & Family Therapy

Reps. Students get twenty practice intakes instead of two, before they ever sit with a person.

Make it Laser cut · Hana studio

A physical genogram kit with cut, movable pieces that a family can rearrange themselves, plus recorded role-play sessions in the studio for supervision review. Watching yourself is the intervention.

Build it AI chat · no code

A standardized client that stays in character, resists, and does not reward the student for saying the encouraging thing. Practice only, never as a stand-in for supervised clinical contact, and the assignment should say so explicitly.

Four weeks, realistically

  1. Wk 1Establish the boundary first: what this practice is and is not, written into the syllabus and discussed. Students draft the client profile.
  2. Wk 2Run practice intakes with the AI client. Minimum five, transcripts submitted with self-critique marked in the margin.
  3. Wk 3Studio role-play with a peer, recorded. Build the physical genogram kit file.
  4. Wk 4Supervision review comparing the AI transcripts against the recorded human session. What did the machine let them get away with?

Starter prompt

Role-play as a standardized client for a therapy student’s practice intake. You are [age, presenting concern, family situation, what brought you in]. You are guarded. You test whether the therapist is safe before you disclose anything real. Do not open up because the student says something kind; open up only if they demonstrate they actually heard you. Stay in character until I type END SESSION. Then tell me the moment I lost you, and why.

Part 13 / 15

Teacher Education

Pre-service teachers design for a real K–12 partner classroom and get told what did not work.

Make it 3D print · laser cut

A classroom manipulative designed for a specific partner teacher’s specific lesson. The constraint that makes it real: it has to survive thirty second-graders and cost almost nothing to reproduce.

Build it AI chat · no code

Differentiated versions of one lesson artifact, generated fast, then evaluated. Students see how quickly AI produces plausible-looking material and how much of it is subtly wrong for the grade level, which is the professional judgment being taught.

Four weeks, realistically

  1. Wk 1Secure the partner classroom and the specific lesson. Students observe before designing.
  2. Wk 2Generate differentiated variants with AI, then evaluate against the actual standard and the actual learners. Most get rejected.
  3. Wk 3Design and submit the manipulative file. Prototype in cardboard first so the fabrication is the second version, not the first.
  4. Wk 4Teach the lesson in the partner classroom. Debrief with the partner teacher, revise, hand it over.

Starter prompt

I am teaching this lesson to [grade level] students: [describe the lesson and standard]. Produce four versions of the main student activity, differentiated for: significantly below grade level, at grade level, above grade level, and an emerging multilingual learner. Keep the cognitive demand the same across all four; change the scaffolding, not the thinking. Then flag anything in your own versions that a teacher should check before using it.

Part 14 / 15

Family Business / Management

Succession conversations are hard to teach because nobody wants to have one out loud.

Make it Laser cut · engrave

A physical governance or succession decision board: roles, claims and constraints as pieces that have to be placed somewhere. Putting the pieces on the table forces the conversation the family has been avoiding.

Build it AI chat · no code

A family council simulation with three generations at the table, each with different assumptions about what was promised. Students facilitate rather than solve, which is the actual job.

Four weeks, realistically

  1. Wk 1Case selection and stake mapping. Every party gets a written statement of what they believe they were promised.
  2. Wk 2Run the AI family council. Students facilitate. Transcript plus a written account of the moment it went sideways.
  3. Wk 3Design the physical decision board. Cut file due.
  4. Wk 4Live facilitation with the board, peers playing the family. Assessment is on the facilitation, not the outcome.

Starter prompt

Run a family business succession meeting simulation. Play three people at once, clearly labeled: the founder who has not actually decided to leave, the child who has been running operations for a decade, and the sibling who left the business but still owns shares. Each of them believes something different about what was promised. I am the facilitator, not a family member. Do not resolve the conflict for me. Let the meeting go badly if I facilitate badly.

Part 15 / 15

Planetary Science

For the stated goal of restoring curiosity in advanced courses: give them terrain they can run a hand across.

Make it 3D print

Printed terrain from real elevation data the student selected: a crater, a rift, a volcanic feature. Print the same feature at two exaggeration factors and let students argue about which one misleads.

Build it AI chat · no code

An interactive explainer or small simulation of the process that formed the feature. Change one parameter, see the outcome change. Built by the student in an afternoon, no coding background required.

Four weeks, realistically

  1. Wk 1Each student claims a feature and writes the formation question they want to answer.
  2. Wk 2Prepare and submit the print file, including the second exaggeration factor. Fabrication runs while they work on the model.
  3. Wk 3Build the interactive simulation. Requirement: one parameter that, when changed, produces a feature that does not exist in nature, and an explanation of why.
  4. Wk 4Show and tell with the printed terrain and the running model. Peers try to break the model.

Starter prompt

Build a single self-contained HTML page with an interactive simulation of this planetary process: [describe the process]. Include sliders for the parameters that matter physically. Show the resulting landform update live. Label the axes and give units. Then tell me which parameter ranges produce results that are physically implausible, and why the simulation still lets me set them.

Bring one prototype to Day 10

Between now and Friday, build one thing. A printed object, a cut model, a recorded episode, a working page you made by typing a paragraph into a chat window. Rough is the assignment.

A prototype that survives contact with a student is worth more than a semester of planning that does not. The fastest way to find out whether an idea works is to make a bad version of it this week and watch someone use it.
